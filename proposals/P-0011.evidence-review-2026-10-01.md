## 1. The summary, as pasted by the founder

Builders should be part of the founding proposition. The feed should demonstrate the framework, with you working under the same terms as future maintainers.**

I read the framework and the preceding exchange. Its direction is stronger, but I wouldn’t adopt its terms unchanged. It still gives builders too little certainty and users too little actual authority.

The opportunity I see is this:

**People keep control of a service. Builders build a livelihood running it.**

That could apply to a volunteer project or a substantial business employing a team. There is no reason for the ambition to stop at small community tools.

Where people already pay for software, the opportunity is to organize that demand around a service they can control. They need a convincing product, a credible operator, and a practical way to move. Builders need a customer relationship worth investing their time in.

That is the exchange our.one should help establish.

**The social graph remains valuable, but shared intent matters too.** Knowing who my friends are doesn’t tell a builder what we need. Knowing that people want a particular service, understand its proposed price, and are prepared to try it is much more useful. our.one could help people express that demand together—and retain their relationship when the software or its operator changes.

I would strengthen the framework in these ways.

**1. Define ownership through decisions people can make.**

The proposed promises—no sale, export, transparency, replacement—are useful. But something important is missing: **who decides the essential rules and the budget?**

Seeing the rules is different from having authority over them. Firing a maintainer is insufficient if their successor can impose the same unwanted terms.

My proposed plain-language definition is:

> Its users, together, decide the service’s essential rules, approve its budget, and can change who runs it while keeping the service going.

The details behind that sentence must establish real rights. Ordinary product decisions can be delegated; people shouldn’t need to vote on every feature. Personal privacy also remains an individual right, outside the majority’s discretion.

This gives “user-owned” a meaning beyond goodwill, transparency, or a future promise.

**2. Make maintainership a desirable professional future.**

The draft currently asks a builder to surrender the possibility of selling the service in exchange for a pitch page, launch exposure, and potential customers. That is a demanding exchange.

A serious maintainer needs something more substantial:

| Users need | Maintainers need |
|---|---|
| An agreed service and operating budget | Clear scope and agreed compensation |
| Authority over important rules | Freedom to make ordinary product decisions |
| The ability to replace the operator | Fair notice, renewal and termination terms |
| Continuity when someone leaves | Attribution and a reputation they can carry forward |

A successful maintainer should be able to earn well, employ people, improve the product, and build a durable operating business. Cheap code should make that more achievable; it shouldn’t become an argument for paying people poorly.

Also, describe the role as **building, improving and running useful software**. “Maintainer” alone can undersell the creativity you want to attract.

Ghost provides a concrete example of paid hosting funding an open-source foundation and professional team. That supports the economic possibility, although it doesn’t establish the user-control model you want. [Ghost’s explanation](https://ghost.org/about/)

**3. Solve the transition from interest to customers.**

The weakest sentence in the draft is:

> “Your backers become your users and pay your public price.”

That step contains much of the problem we are trying to solve.

An email address means someone wants to hear more. A builder needs to know what people would actually choose:

- What problem do they want solved?
- What would they need to move?
- Would they try this particular offer?
- Would they pay its proposed price?

A pitch should answer those questions before its counter becomes impressive. Interest, trial participation, and willingness to pay should remain visibly different.

Builders should be able to propose solutions. People should also be able to gather around a need and invite proposals. That makes participation useful even for someone who has no interest in the feed.

The research supports concern about difficult app economics. It doesn’t confirm that our.one will solve distribution: RevenueCat’s findings concern a defined sample of subscription apps, not every builder’s prospects. [Report and methodology](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)

**4. Replace delisting with a credible continuity mechanism.**

Your earlier concern about unreliable apps and exposed data remains central.

If a maintainer controls the accounts, database, domain and deployment, removing their listing does little for the people depending on them. **The meaningful test is whether another appointed maintainer can actually continue the service.**

That requires appropriate rights, recovery arrangements, and access to the necessary assets. Builders receiving scoped permissions instead of unrestricted network data is part of this protection.

Independent experiments can be listed honestly. They shouldn’t inherit stronger privacy or control claims merely by signing a promise.

This is also an opportunity to give builders something valuable: shared operating capabilities that reduce what they must manage themselves, as those capabilities become available.

**5. Reconsider the global threshold and permanent economic promises.**

I would separate each service’s route to user control from the feed’s **100,000-account threshold**. A paid service could become important to its users before the wider network reaches that size. Its rights need a credible route to becoming operative.

That means revisiting an adopted decision, but the proposed product has changed substantially enough to justify examining it.

I also wouldn’t promise “our.one never takes a cut” yet. I would promise **public, approved shared costs**. Identity, recovery, support and oversight need funding. Their allocation should follow the actual responsibilities.

Likewise, the draft’s revenue illustration demonstrates possible gross revenue, not maintainer income. Professional compensation, infrastructure, support and reserves must fit inside it.

For the public message, I would use this direction:

We’re building our.one so people can bring the software they want into existence—and keep control of it.

Builders can earn a living creating and running those services under clear agreements with their users.

The feed is our first project. We’re inviting builders and future users to shape what comes next.

I would launch that proposition alongside a readable common agreement, the feed’s project page, and a concrete route for aspiring maintainers. I would seek an independently led project with a plausible substantial market and prospective users who can evaluate a real offer.

The next proof worth pursuing is **a capable builder choosing these terms, people choosing their service, and enough continuing revenue to make maintaining it worthwhile**. Demonstrating that the service can survive a change of maintainer would then make the ownership promise tangible.

I saved the [full review and proposed terms](docs-internal/the-framework.review-2026-10-01.md). It remains a proposal; no implementation or adopted commitments were changed.

## 2. The full review

# The framework — a reciprocal agreement

**Status: PROPOSED.** An independent response to the founder's question and
`the-framework.md` draft 0.2, prepared on 1 October 2026. Editorial and product
judgment, not adopted rights, a BUILD mandate, a legal instrument, or evidence
of demand. No implementation or existing decision is changed.

## My view

The founder is right to put builders in the founding proposition. My earlier
recommendation to give them a secondary contribution link was too limited
for this ambition. The feed can demonstrate the relationship without
defining the limits of the institution.

The framework draft moves in the right direction, but its current exchange
is uneven. Builders give up an important source of potential financial
upside in return for a listing and possible attention. Users receive promises
without clear authority over important rules and spending. Delisting is the
main remedy, while an outside operator still holds the service.

The opportunity is to organize people who want useful software into a
customer that can commission and sustain it, and give capable builders a
credible livelihood serving that customer. Repeated across services, the
shared rights, relationships, operating support and trust could become
valuable. That is a hypothesis to prove through use and payment, not an
audience the project already has.

## The proposition

> We're building our.one so people can bring the software they want into
> existence and keep control of it. Builders can earn a living creating and
> running services for those people. The feed is our first project; the same
> agreement should guide what comes next.

The public story needs the user benefit and the builder opportunity together.
For prospective maintainers, explain the creative and operating role: build,
improve and run a useful service, with a route to being paid. “Maintainer”
must not make the offer sound confined to fixing somebody else's bugs.

## A stronger definition of user ownership

The draft's no-sale, exit, transparency and replacement promises are valuable.
They do not explicitly give users authority over the service's essential
rules or its budget. Seeing a rule change is different from having authority
to accept or reject it. Replacing an operator is not enough if the next
operator can impose the same unwanted terms.

My proposed public definition is:

> Its users, together, decide the service's essential rules, approve its
> budget, and can change who runs it while keeping the service going.

Under that sentence, the common agreement should establish:

- **Collective authority:** a defined membership and a practical decision
  process for purpose, important rules, budgets and appointments. Ordinary
  product work is delegated; users are not asked to vote on each change.
- **Continuity:** the community has the necessary rights and access to keep
  operating with a replacement, covering the code licence, service name,
  domain, permitted data, recovery, administration and funds.
- **Individual rights:** collective decisions do not turn personal data or
  private relationships into assets the majority may expose or sell.
- **Limits on sale and capture:** the holder cannot sell away the shared
  control and continuity rights that the agreement promises.

Open records and portable data make these rights inspectable and useful.
They are not substitutes for the rights. “User-owned” should describe an
actual arrangement once established; a project working toward it should say
which rights are still promises.

This is a proposed operational definition, not a conclusion about legal
ownership. It does not require speculative or tradable interests. Its legal
instruments must actually confer the rights claimed.

## Maintainers need protection too

A capable builder should be able to understand the working arrangement
before committing to it:

| Users receive | Maintainers receive |
|---|---|
| A defined service and important rules they control | A defined scope and autonomy to do the work |
| An agreed operating budget and visible compensation | Agreed pay once funded, a term and a renewal process |
| A right to change the operator | Notice, fair termination terms and a dispute process |
| A service that can survive an operator's departure | Attribution and the opportunity to build a professional reputation |

Healthy compensation, teams and reserves are compatible with this model.
Reducing software costs should not create a duty to work indefinitely for
free. A founder can volunteer; that is a declared funding choice for that
project, not the implied employment model for the ecosystem.

The builder may retain copyright while granting the rights needed to
continue the service. An open licence does not by itself secure the service
name: Apache-2.0, used in the repository, separately excludes a general
trademark grant. The agreement must cover the assets that matter in a real
handover. [Apache-2.0, sections 2 and 6](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0)

My preferred builder offer is therefore: **help organize demand for your
work, agree a sustainable operating budget, and earn recurring income for
serving that community under fair terms.** Until those facilities and
customers exist, describe this as the founding offer being developed.

## Replace email enthusiasm with a path to commitment

“Your backers become your users and pay your public price” is the weakest
step in the draft. It assumes the central uncertainty away. A verified
address establishes interest in hearing more; it does not establish a
person's identity, adoption, payment or governance standing.

A proposal should make the decision concrete:

- the need and the experience the builder proposes;
- what a user would need to change or migrate;
- the proposed price, scope and operating budget, including maintainer pay;
- what support is sought now: feedback, a trial, or a conditional intention
  to buy;
- what must happen before work or paid operation begins, and what happens
  if it does not.

Keep expressions of interest distinct from commitments. A conditional
intention to buy is still weaker than a payment. Any actual collection of
funds needs clear terms, consent and an appropriate payment arrangement;
this review does not propose taking money now.

Volunteer and free services can use the same rights agreement with a named
funding source. Funding is essential before promising a paid job, not a
mandatory toll before anyone may publish a prototype.

Both directions should be possible: a builder proposes a service, or people
gather around an unmet need and invite builders to make an offer. Multiple
offers can address the same need. Participation grants neither automatic
access to the network nor authority over its members.

## The economics need fewer permanent promises

The draft's illustration is arithmetically correct:

> 1,000,000 users × 10% paying × €24/year = €2,400,000/year gross revenue.

These are hypothetical inputs, not a forecast. Gross revenue must cover
applicable taxes and fees, infrastructure, support, security, maintenance,
shared services and reserves. It is not the maintainer's personal income.
The difficult assumptions are attracting and retaining the paying people
and operating at an affordable total cost.

“No investors” does not make customer revenue arrive on the first day.
Likewise, “our.one never takes a cut” promises a funding structure before
the cost of its responsibilities is known. I would propose the principle
**shared costs are public and approved**, and decide the allocation from
actual services and costs. A percentage is not inherently extractive; a
flat fee is not inherently fair. Existing transparent fiscal hosting uses
fees to fund administration and other services.
[Open Collective's explanation](https://documentation.opencollective.com/why-open-collective/pricing)

Users can fund worthwhile work without giving its provider a permanent
right to control or sell their shared service. That separation is the
economic proposition to make attractive.

## Delisting cannot be the ownership mechanism

If an operator holds the accounts, data, name and production access, removing
its listing leaves users depending on that operator. A consented export is
useful, but continuity also requires a capable successor and authority to
run the service.

The decisive test is: **can the appointed successor actually continue the
service, with the permitted records and relationships, without the former
maintainer having a veto?** Also test whether the community can continue if
the operator of our.one itself refuses to cooperate.

Listing an independent experiment is possible if it is clearly described as
such. It must not inherit the privacy or control claims of a protected
our.one service. A named maintainer and public promises are not sufficient
defences against insecure code.

For services entrusted with shared data, access must be scoped, revocable
and checked; operations need recovery and continuity arrangements. Builders
do not receive the network's address book or unrestricted data access as a
membership benefit. Build the required protections for concrete services,
then generalize what has actually worked.

## The feed should follow the agreement, not gate the ecosystem

Show it as the first project: its purpose, current maintainer, public budget,
voluntary unpaid work, current control and remaining transfer commitments.
The same questions should be asked of subsequent projects.

Do not make someone use a social feed to express demand for a different
service. Common identity and rights can support different entry points;
participation in an app and permission to contact someone remain choices.

I would revisit making actual member control depend on the network reaching
100,000 accounts. A funded service could become important to its users
before the network reaches that size. Its rights should have an explicit
route to becoming operative that is appropriate to the service, not be left
dependent on unrelated growth. The present threshold is an adopted
commitment; changing its role requires a new human decision. This review
does not silently replace it or claim that rights already exist.

One common agreement can cover different sizes and funding models, with a
short project schedule naming its constituency, operator, budget and assets.
Common rights need not mean one worldwide electorate deciding each app's
features or one undifferentiated database available to its builders.

## What I would launch

Launch the shared proposition and the maintainer route alongside the feed.
The founder should seek an independently led project whose prospective
users can evaluate a real offer. It should have room to serve a substantial
market, while its initial scope remains something the maintainer can
actually deliver and support.

The first public framework can be small:

- a readable common agreement covering user and maintainer rights;
- project pages showing the need, proposed experience, operator, funding and
  current control, with the feed under the same questions;
- an invitation for builders to propose a service and for people to express
  a specific need;
- a manually supported route from a proposal to a trial and a funded
  operating agreement.

Do not imply those pages already supply an audience, a reliable runtime,
member authority or guaranteed income. The proof to seek is an independent
builder choosing these terms, users choosing the resulting service, funded
maintenance being renewed, and a credible demonstration that the service
can outlast its current operator. A catalogue of pitches alone would not
prove the model.

## Evidence and limits

- The review reads `the-framework.md` and `the-maintainers.md` as drafts,
  alongside the founder's supplied exchange. It also checks THESIS §7–11,
  THE-LAYER's control, runtime and builder sections, the institution compiler,
  AGENTS.md, and the current D-0016 direction. No source draft is amended.
- RevenueCat's report supports the concern about difficult, uneven app
  outcomes. Its sample comprises apps using RevenueCat with active
  subscription revenue and minimum activity thresholds; its median is not
  the success probability of every person building software. It does not
  establish that our.one can aggregate paying demand.
  [Report and methodology](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)
- Ghost describes paid hosting funding its open-source foundation and team.
  This supports the possibility of a professional service-funded model. It
  does not establish democratic user control or prove a multi-app ecosystem.
  [Ghost's explanation](https://ghost.org/about/)
- The proposed separation of service users' authority from professional
  operation has established relatives. The ICA definition combines shared
  ownership with democratic control. This review claims no invention of
  cooperatives and makes no jurisdiction-specific legal determination.
  [ICA principles](https://ica.coop/en/cooperatives/cooperative-identity)
- External pages were checked by the assistant during this review. They
  are inputs to a proposal; public use still needs the repository's human
  source confirmation. Demand, conversion, operating economics and the
  effectiveness of the proposed agreement remain untested here.
