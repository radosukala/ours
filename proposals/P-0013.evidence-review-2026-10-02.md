# A front door for the whole idea

**PROPOSED · 2 October 2026 · design and product review.**
This is a recommendation, not an adopted decision or implementation mandate.
The accompanying [interactive design study](prototype/our-one-next.html)
does not change the application, submit proposals, create accounts or confer
rights. Examples are labelled; the maintainer replacement is an illustration.

## My view

The underlying project has become substantially more concrete. The
presentation has not caught up. Someone arriving at the homepage is invited
to try a quiet social feed. Someone opening the builder page encounters a
long explanation of rules and limitations. Neither arrival makes the whole
opportunity easy to grasp.

The strongest proposition is **people coming together to create software
they can continue to control, with a credible livelihood for the people
who look after it**. The feed makes that tangible. The kit makes building
under common terms tangible. Both belong inside the same invitation.

The kit is meaningful progress. I ran version 0.2.0 against `apps/web`:
its ten checks passed. The report also says `pitch: missing` and lists
runtime and custody safeguards as not built. This verifies the checker’s
current result, not service safety, admission, an audience or ownership.
The [verification record](../receipts/conformance/2026-10-02-M-0016.verification.md)
documents the earlier independent trial and fixes. I did not rerun the
application’s full suite or independently audit the checker.

## What is holding the invitation back

**The homepage still defines the project as the feed.**
`FrontPage.tsx` leads with “Just your people. Then you’re done.” That is a
useful project promise. It is too narrow for the institution’s front door.
The public header offers the brand and sign-in; the builder path is in the
footer. This follows the current decisions, but makes the larger direction
hard to discover. The proposed design puts projects, building and public
records in the main navigation, and puts both audiences in the first screen.

**The visual language makes every idea look like documentation.**
The existing public pages inherit the feed’s system type, colours, borders
and narrow prose column. This is a coherent implementation, but it gives
the invitation little identity of its own. The proposal uses a warm paper
surface, dark green ink, an acid green field, a restrained rust accent,
large editorial type and an authored diagram. It shows a public project
with a point of view. The signed-in feed need not inherit this presentation.

**The builder journey still reproduces “build first, find people later.”**
`kit/build.md` orders the work as questions, setup, declarations, build,
check, then proposal. That is sensible for technical preparation. It is a
poor requirement for finding out whether an idea should be built.

Separate **an early idea** from **a project ready for technical review**.
A builder should be able to put a short offer in front of potential users
before completing code. An existing project should enter at the appropriate
stage. The kit becomes useful when preparing the build and trial. The
prototype offers an early idea draft alongside the agent starter prompt.
This is a proposed flow change, not a claim that an idea has passed the kit.

**The message test is testing the old product definition.**
[The current protocol](the-message-test.md) counts understanding primarily
as “a social network or feed for your friends”; its wider variants still
need an association with people or friends. It also treats “nobody owns it”
as evidence of differentiation. Those criteria can favour the narrow pitch
and reward a misunderstanding of collective ownership.

The new comprehension question is whether someone understands a place where
people and builders can create services under user control, with the feed
as the first project. Ask what users would control, what builders would
receive, and what the visitor thinks is available today. Test with both
potential users and builders. A small qualitative test can expose confusion;
it cannot establish that a movement will spread.

## The message I would test

> **The software we live in should be ours.**
>
> We’re bringing people and builders together to create services their users
> can control. Starting with a friends feed. Building toward much more.

The supporting thought is: **AI helps us build. Together, we can make it
ours.** It names a choice we can act on, without needing the claim that code
or reliable operation is free.

For builders, lead with the work and the opportunity: **Build something
people can depend on.** Then make the deal concrete: users who choose and
fund the service can support agreed pay for a maintainer or team; the
builder receives scope, autonomy and fair terms, while the service can
continue under user authority. An audience and income remain unproven.

I would retire the current “on your own” comparison’s absolutes. Independent
builders can also work with users, publish open source and arrange
succession. our.one should explain its own proposed deal rather than
caricature their alternatives.

The differentiation also needs to go beyond a shared social graph. For
example, the [AT Protocol identity guide](https://atproto.com/guides/identity)
already describes retaining identity, graph and content across providers.
My inference: our.one’s stronger emphasis is the combination of collective
authority, paid maintenance and continuity under common terms. This is a
positioning recommendation, not a claim of unique invention.

## The participation mechanism matters as much as the page

An email address says little about what someone will use. A builder with
a rules file still needs people who want the work.

The first loop can be run by hand:

1. A person describes a service they depend on, what should change, and
   what would make an alternative worth trying.
2. A builder describes an idea, who it would serve and how they would find
   out whether those people want it. Code is optional at this stage.
3. With consent, the founder connects related needs and offers. A concrete
   proposal states the scope, migration burden, operating costs, maintainer
   pay and conditions for a trial. Interest, trial participation and payment
   remain separate.
4. A promising project uses the kit and receives human review before the
   relevant trial. Passing a static scan does not authorize access to people’s
   data; the adopted safeguards still govern any protected operation.

That creates a reason to bring another person: “We want this. Would you
actually try it with us?” It also creates a reason for builders to bring
their own constituency. It does not assume our.one already has an audience
to distribute to them. A manual loop does not limit the eventual market;
it makes the proposed exchange inspectable before automating it.

The next proof I would prioritise is an independent builder responding to
an identifiable need under these terms, followed by actual use. The next
technical foundation is a narrow working example of protected access and
continuity. More patterns in a checker cannot substitute for that boundary.

## What the design study does

- Makes the wider invitation the headline, with two visible entrances.
- Shows the feed as the first project and labels work/creator examples as
  possibilities, not a fabricated catalogue.
- Demonstrates the intended meaning of replacement: change the maintainer,
  retain the people and agreed rules. It explicitly says this is not an
  operative or tested transfer.
- Shows the real builder kit, its limitations, the conditional pay model,
  and a proposed earlier idea entrance. The repository starter prompt is
  new suggested wording; it has not had its own independent agent trial.
- Gives current evidence its own readable section, with links to code,
  costs, the draft agreement and authority records.
- Lets a visitor draft a need or an idea locally. Copy buttons use the
  clipboard. There is no submission, account, analytics, persistent storage
  or automatic external request. Explicit source links open GitHub.

This is a proposed public identity. Its colours, headline, diagrams and
flows have not been adopted or tested with strangers. Public factual claims
still require the repository’s human source confirmation before release.

## Proposed implementation mandate for human review

**Status: PROPOSED. Authority: none issued. Class requested: BUILD.**

**Outcome:** the public front door explains the whole user/maintainer
proposition and leads each audience to an honest, usable next step.

**Preconditions:** resolve the revised message test and adopt the selected
copy and visual direction. D-0018 §C says “The front page’s headline and
lede stay as they are until the five-stranger test is run”; §“Prohibited”
also forbids changing them before that test. D-0019 and M-0016 preserve
that boundary. This proposal does not bypass it. The earlier idea route
and any change to agent instructions need an explicit scope decision too.

**Proposed scope:** public navigation, homepage presentation, the feed’s
project presentation, builder-page hierarchy, and an idea/need draft flow
that opens an email only when the existing configured address is available.
Preserve the existing account, invitation and privacy gates. No marketplace,
new collection database, payments, legal claims or runtime-control changes.

**Acceptance:** newcomer comprehension covers the wider proposition and
current status; users can name a next step; builders can distinguish early
ideas from checked projects; links and conditional routes work; examples
cannot be mistaken for real demand; keyboard and mobile use remain clear.
Source checks and application checks apply to the implemented change.

**Deployment:** separate authority. Nothing in this review authorizes it.
