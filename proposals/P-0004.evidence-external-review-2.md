**Yes. I think this is a stronger direction: OURS helps people turn a
shared need into software they can collectively sustain and control.**

It gives the institution compiler a human purpose people can understand. It
also changes your role as founder: you are building the conditions under
which other people can build responsibly together.

My proposed product sentence would be:

> **OURS helps communities create and run software of their own.**
>
> Bring a shared need. Adapt what exists or build what's missing. Decide how
> it works, see what it costs, and keep control as it grows.

The movement remains:

> **The software we live in should be OURS.**

The distinction matters. The movement expresses what we believe. The product
gives someone something useful to do.

**The basic unit should be a shared need.** People should be able to arrive
before they have a formal community, a specification, or a builder. Imagine
someone saying: "We share a workshop. Booking equipment happens in messages,
maintenance gets forgotten, and access rules depend on who you ask." That
is an excellent starting point. There are identifiable people, recurring
use, concrete consequences, and rules worth deciding together. The first
OURS screen could ask: **What do you need to do together?** Then help them
describe: who experiences the problem; how they handle it today; what a
better outcome would look like; who is willing to try it with them. The
output is a simple invitation others can join: **"We need this too."** That
expresses interest, not ownership or voting rights. Crucially, OURS should
help them discover an existing solution before commissioning another
application. Sometimes the right result is adopting software, configuring
it, or funding an improvement to a shared component. **Successful
coordination should count as success even when it produces little new
code.** That is how you avoid becoming an endless gallery of generated apps.

**The product should feel like a home for a shared tool.** I would organize
the experience around a few ordinary questions:

| What people see | What it answers |
|---|---|
| **Use** | Where is our working tool? |
| **Improve** | What needs to change, and what is happening about it? |
| **People & rules** | Who belongs, who decides, and what protects us? |
| **Money** | What does this cost, who pays, and who gets paid? |
| **Continuity** | Who runs it, and what happens if they leave? |

Most people should spend their time using the tool. Participation becomes
relevant when a decision affects them. They should not need to understand
mandates, deployment digests, or constitutional projections. A change can
simply say: **Booking cancellation rule** — proposed by the workshop
coordinator; affects people waiting for equipment; open for discussion;
estimated cost and implementation plan available. After release, the same
record explains what changed and whether it worked. The technical evidence
sits underneath that explanation.

**Ownership should be understandable through actions.** "Community-owned"
becomes concrete when a person can answer: Can we replace the people
running this? Can someone sell it or change its purpose without our
agreement? Can we challenge a decision? What can we take with us if we
leave? Who controls our information? A percentage, badge, or membership
certificate is insufficient. I would make those answers visible in the
product, including unresolved ones. During formation, it might say:
**Currently operated by the founding team. Community control has not yet
transferred. These steps remain.** A guided process can make formation
easier, but it must not disguise an unfinished ownership arrangement as a
completed one.

**I would change the promise of "cannot be bypassed."** The ambition is
right. Absolute protection from takeover, scams, privacy breaches, or data
loss is not a promise we can honestly make. The stronger product
requirement is to identify the powers that could betray the community and
constrain them explicitly: builders receive bounded access — writing code
does not grant ownership or deployment authority; operators have defined
responsibilities, replacement procedures, and limited emergency powers;
changes to purpose, ownership, or sensitive data use require the
appropriate authority; backups, recovery, and operator handover are
exercised; personal information stays private even when decisions and
spending are inspectable; remaining override powers are visible. A
community majority must also have limits. Collective ownership does not
entitle members to another person's private data or remove the need for
appeal. **The promise should be meaningful control, backed by specific
protections and a credible path to recovery.** Each protection needs an
honest account of what holds it: software, separated responsibilities,
legal arrangements, or an expectation that is still only written down.

**You need a protocol, but people should encounter a service.** The
underlying protocol could describe a durable agreement among: the people
the software serves; the people authorized to decide; the builders and
operators; the organizations holding assets or funds; the software and
evidence connecting their actions. Its practical test is portability:
**could another capable operator continue serving this community using
these records, assets, and permissions?** That is more important than
publishing an elaborate specification early. There is relevant prior art.
PolicyKit supports executable community governance procedures; Open
Collective provides infrastructure through which groups can operate with
fiscal hosts. Those show useful parts of the problem already being
addressed. OURS would need to connect those kinds of capabilities to useful
software and its ongoing control — not claim to have invented collective
governance. (Reviewer's links: policykit.org; opencollective.com
fiscal-hosting.) I would develop the protocol through an assisted founding
experience, then extract the parts that repeat.

**Share the building blocks; keep authority local.** Your concern about
fragmented copies is important. I would distinguish a **shared software
family** from the **communities using it**. Several workshops could use
the same booking software, share maintenance costs, and benefit from
improvements. Each workshop could retain separate membership, private
data, budgets, and local operating rules. A common improvement could be
offered to participating communities. Consequential changes would follow
their agreed adoption procedures. Routine maintenance could happen under
previously delegated authority. This creates a compelling economic
possibility: **We can share the cost of software without surrendering
control of our community.** That is a hypothesis worth testing. It also
exposes a necessary boundary: a community needing an unusual feature may
need to fund its additional maintenance. Shared infrastructure does not
make divergent needs costless. Forking remains a continuity right. Reuse
should usually be the easier practical choice.

**Builders need a respected, sustainable role.** I would welcome builders
as paid contributors and operators working under clear agreements. They can
earn money for creating something useful, maintaining it, improving shared
components, and providing dependable service. Their work should have
attribution, clear acceptance criteria, and a fair dispute process. The
community also has responsibilities: choosing priorities, funding agreed
work, responding to decisions, and treating contributors fairly. Otherwise
OURS risks becoming a place where people expect unlimited custom software
from volunteers. Removing the incentive to sell a company does not remove
the need to reward skilled work. I would postpone an open builder
marketplace. First learn how to make a community–builder relationship work
with deliberately matched people. A marketplace would introduce its own
trust and quality problems before you have a proven operating model.

**What I would build first.** I would build an assisted **"start something
together"** experience around a real shared tool: (1) describe the need and
invite affected people; (2) evaluate existing software and reusable
components; (3) agree on a short, readable founding arrangement; (4)
identify a builder, operator, budget, and bounded first outcome; (5)
deliver the tool through the existing authority chain; (6) make a real
improvement together; (7) rehearse replacing the operator. The founding
arrangement should answer purpose, participation, decisions, money,
privacy, and continuity. It should use guided choices and explain
consequences. Material ambiguity still needs human judgment. The first
success would be a group saying: **"We use this. We understand what it
costs. We know how to change it. Its future does not depend entirely on the
person who built it."** I would treat that as the test of the product.

**My recommendation is to make OURS the shared home for forming,
improving, and sustaining community software — with a portable protocol
underneath it.** Keep the existing kernel as infrastructure. Redirect the
proposed consumer experience toward the life of a shared tool. The most
important simplification is that people should not have to become
institution designers to exercise meaningful control. OURS should make the
necessary decisions understandable, support competent delegation, and let
people get back to the activity they came together for.
