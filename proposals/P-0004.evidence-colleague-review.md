Yes—this is closely aligned, and it sharpens the idea in a useful way:

> **"The ownership layer is the centre, and the compiler is its gate."**

That is the strongest sentence in the document. I would keep it as the
central **proposed direction**.

It explains why our previous attempts felt wrong: we were organizing the
experience around producing and administering applications. Your ambition
is to establish something that people retain while applications change.

**The colleague adds something important: a concrete definition of an
app.**

An app becomes a replaceable implementation with a contract specifying what
it may do, which information it may access, and whose authority permits it.

That connects the institutional thesis to a technical boundary. Ownership
stops being something explained beside the product and starts determining
how the product operates.

I also agree with using existing coding systems. OURS should define the
authorized outcome and the conditions for running it. Building its own
general coding factory would distract from the harder, more distinctive
work.

However, I would strengthen this draft before promoting it into the
foundation.

**"The layer" must mean a durable relationship, not necessarily a central
database or a single holder.**

The document moves from shared ownership infrastructure to "one holder
inherited by every Cell," calling that simpler and stronger. That conclusion
is premature.

A common constitution and interoperable infrastructure could support
different holders and operators. Personal information, community assets,
root governance, and operating funds need not sit under identical control.

The requirement should be:

> **A person or community can replace software and operators while
> preserving their legitimate rights and continuity.**

The holding architecture should be judged against that requirement.
Centralization might simplify an initial implementation; it does not
establish stronger ownership.

**"The data cannot be taken" is too strong—even within the proposed
architecture.**

Scoped access limits exposure. It does not establish that an app cannot
misuse information it is authorized to read.

The draft needs to distinguish permission to access information from
permission—and technical ability—to transmit, retain, or repurpose it.
Runtime isolation, outbound access restrictions, minimal disclosure, and
operator controls belong in that account.

Similarly, a public governance record can contain private information.
Transparency needs deliberate public projections, rather than a default
that proposals, decisions, and observations are public in their entirety.

These corrections strengthen your ambition by identifying what the
protective layer must actually hold.

**The swap requires a durable definition of the service.**

"Another app satisfies the same charter" is insufficient to establish that
it can replace the current one. A charter could permit applications with
incompatible meanings for a booking, permission, document, or pending
transaction.

OURS needs an authoritative service specification covering the meaning of
records, expected behavior, permissions, and transition requirements.
Otherwise, replacing the code could preserve the database while breaking
what the data means.

This is where Spec Kit becomes particularly useful: the specification
persists alongside the institution, while implementations can vary.
Regeneration should preserve agreed behavior and state through a verified
transition; it need not mean frequently rewriting functioning software.

**There is another implication I would make explicit: technical admission
does not grant a constituency.**

A builder passing conformance should earn eligibility to offer an
application. The relevant people must still authorize its use and access.

That gives OURS a clear relationship with builders:

> **Build to our requirements. Offer us something useful. Receive the
> permissions and compensation we agree to.**

It also protects OURS from becoming an app marketplace whose commercial
objective gradually turns its members into an audience sold to suppliers.

The draft's strongest demonstration is indeed **the swap**. I would make it
the architectural test:

> Can the community replace the implementation and its supplier while
> retaining its rights, permitted records, relationships, decisions, and
> ability to operate?

That test reaches beyond a convincing interface into the actual allocation
of power.

I would carry forward this synthesis:

> **OURS is the enduring institution people belong to. Applications are
> replaceable services operating under its members' authority. The compiler
> connects their decisions to verified changes; the runtime holds the
> resulting permissions.**

This preserves the scale of your ambition. The next foundational work is to
define what persists, who controls each part, and what replacement must
preserve. Those answers should determine the software architecture—and then
the interface.
