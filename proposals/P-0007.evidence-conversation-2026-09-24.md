## 3. Colleague reviewer

> I would change my recommendation: start with identity and connections.
> […]
> We're building a shared home for our connections, controlled by the
> people who use it. Connect with your friends and follow the people you
> choose, keeping those connections as you change compatible apps.
> Together, we set the rules and sustain the infrastructure. […]
> Someone shares a personal link: "Connect with me on OURS." […]
> Friendship requires mutual acceptance; following is a subscriber's
> choice. Private connections stay private. […]
> OURS operates identity, connections, permissions, storage, recovery, and
> delivery. […] Builders contribute experiences through limited, reviewed
> interfaces. […] Notification delivery belongs to the shared service. […]
> once an application receives private information, revoking access cannot
> prove it discarded its copy. […]
> You control your connections and permissions. Participants govern the
> shared rules and can replace its operators. The community can continue if
> its founder leaves. […]
> I would also keep "cents per year" out of the promise until measured. […]
> AT Protocol explicitly designs for identity, social graph, and content to
> survive a provider change. […] portability alone is established prior
> art. […]
> I would […] describe the problem through incentives and lost control
> rather than claim the law forces addictive design.

## 5. Colleague reviewer — on the message, money and control

> OURS
> Stay connected. On our terms.
> A home for friends and people you choose to follow. Their posts, in
> order, with an end when you're caught up.
> We're building OURS so our connections can stay with us as apps
> change—and the people using it can control its future.
> Open code. Public costs. Clear rules.
> Connect with me on OURS.
> […]
> I would also hold back "Not for sale" as a present-tense claim. Having no
> outside investors does not prevent a founder from selling. Becoming a
> public benefit corporation does not, by itself, prevent a sale or give
> users control.
> […] A simple public costs page could answer: What money has actually been
> contributed? What has been spent, with redacted supporting records? What
> remains available? What bills and commitments are coming next?
> Your example of $2,500 in founder funding belongs there once recorded as
> received; before that, it is a proposed commitment. Likewise, $700 per
> year for the domain should become a published expense only when the
> registrar's invoice confirms it. Unpaid founder work can be acknowledged
> separately, without inventing a monetary valuation.
> Keep an OURS ledger separate from the company's other projects. Show
> whether founder funding is repayable, and distinguish paid costs from
> estimates and provider credits. […]
> For public contributions, I would use "Support OURS" or "Help cover the
> running costs." We should not imply charitable tax deductibility unless
> the recipient actually qualifies. […]
> Ctrl AI, Inc. is a proposed starting operator, not the ownership mechanism
> by itself. […] Its authority, asset holdings, and responsibility for OURS
> should be verified and recorded before publishing those details. […] a
> Delaware PBC remains a for-profit corporation […]; conversion does not
> automatically issue rights to users. […]
> Founder-led and founder-funded at launch. Working toward control by the
> people using it.
> The eventual arrangement needs to answer who can change the rules,
> appoint operators, sell essential assets, and authorize continuity if the
> founder leaves. […]
> Transparency should cover power alongside code and money. A readable page
> showing who currently controls the domain, infrastructure, releases, and
> funds would make the transition visible as it happens. Published source
> should also identify the version actually running.
> I would use OURS as the name and `our.one` as the product address. […]
> Keep `oursorg.com` as the institutional home for the records. […]
> mobile-ready web, then iOS, then Android, sharing the same core service.
> […] Reporting, blocking, and responsive support belong in the shared
> foundation; Apple explicitly requires these capabilities for social apps.
