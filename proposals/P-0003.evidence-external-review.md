**Model:** not named in the record — `[CONFIRM]`. The founder described it

**Status of this text:** deliberation input. It is quoted so that the

I would keep the ambition of OURS and change its entry point: help a real
group of people take control of a useful piece of their digital life, then
make that success repeatable.

The institution compiler is a strong technical thesis. But the invitation
people should hear is: *The software we live in should be OURS. We're
building a way for the people who depend on software to shape it, sustain
it, and ultimately control it. Bring a community. Choose something worth
changing. Build it together.* That is a proposed shift, not an adopted
change to the project.

**Where you actually are.** You are before the consumer product build, but
not before code. The repository contains the kernel, CLI, verifier, proof
application, and build receipts. The adopted next step is a professional
feed with personal controls, explained defaults, and settings connected to
their governing records. Its build mandate is adopted; the feed application
is absent from the checkout. The institution remains founder-controlled.
Membership and ownership have not been issued, and the founding authority
declaration still carries draft status. Earlier decisions also remain
marked draft. That is an unresolved credibility issue for a project whose
central promise is legitimate authority. I inspected the records, not live
production or runtime conformance.

Your plan already understands that mechanism alone does not attract users.
My disagreement is with the proposed answer: a familiar feed whose
strongest difference lives in its settings. A feed asks people to establish
a new habit, supply content, and find people worth following. Explained
settings are valuable, but I would not bet the movement on them overcoming
that burden.

**The shift I would make.** I would make OURS a way for existing
communities to commission and govern shared software. Start with a
deliberately selected professional circle: independent designers,
developers, researchers, or another group you can personally reach. People
with relationships, recurring needs, and enough shared purpose to make
decisions together. Give them a useful home for something they already do
badly across scattered tools — for example, finding collaborators and
exchanging trusted work opportunities. The initial experience might be: a
portable page showing what I do, what I need, and whether I am available; a
useful, finite view of opportunities and requests from my circle;
introductions and responses that lead to actual collaboration; clear
control over visibility and notifications; a way to propose a consequential
improvement and see its disposition. The exact workflow is a hypothesis to
test with that circle. I would start with work opportunities because the
outcome can be concrete: a useful introduction, a collaborator found, a
project staffed. A chronological feed could support this. It would serve
the workflow rather than define the product.

The deeper proposition is: *We can change this place because we are the
people it exists to serve.* That becomes meaningful when an actual request
becomes a working change, with its costs, tradeoffs, authority, and result
visible.

**What users should experience.** Imagine a participant says: "I keep
missing relevant requests. But I don't want another app competing for my
attention." OURS helps the group examine the problem. Perhaps the answer is
a scheduled digest, an explicit availability setting, or a limited view of
unanswered requests. A steward prepares a bounded proposal. People with the
appropriate authority decide. Agents help implement it. Someone verifies
it. The approved change is released and evaluated. The participant sees:
*You raised this problem. Here is what changed. Here is why. Here is what it
cost. Here is how to challenge it.* During bootstrap, that must explicitly
say the founder made the decision. Later, the same interaction should
reflect rights people actually hold. This is where "OURS" becomes an
experience rather than a claim. Keep the detailed authority trace available
underneath. The main interface should explain the human consequence first.

**How I would challenge incumbents.** I would avoid positioning around a
feature they can reproduce. Bluesky already provides custom feeds and
algorithmic choice. Social.coop already operates a cooperative social
community. Mastodon is pursuing nonprofit institutional structures. These
are substantial adjacent efforts, and potential collaborators — not
evidence of an empty market. (Reviewer's links: bsky.social custom-feeds
post of 27 July 2023; wiki.social.coop; blog.joinmastodon.org
administrative update of February 2026.) I would also challenge a claim in
the adopted decision: incumbents can copy settings with receipts. That
interface does not inherently require transferring control. The harder
distinction is whether people can: replace the operator; constrain changes
to the product's purpose; inspect spending and choose what to sustain;
challenge a decision through a meaningful appeal; continue elsewhere with
usable software and permitted data. OURS should compete on usefulness
backed by credible control. The strategic opportunity is to serve bounded
communities whose needs do not justify the compromises of a mass-market
product. A focused professional circle may want fewer posts, fewer
notifications, and a faster path to useful work. OURS can make those
outcomes its explicit purpose. Its advantage would have to accumulate
through competent operation, relationships, fair decisions, and successful
transfers of control. Copyable code is compatible with that advantage.

**How the movement could grow.** I would recruit people who can bring an
existing practice, not merely endorse an idea. The best early participants
are people who can say: "We already help each other find work. Here is
where our current tools fail us. We will try a better way." Give them a
founding project, not a founding rank. Early participation should create
work, evidence, and responsibility; it should not permanently entrench the
earliest arrivals. The invitation becomes: *Bring something your community
already does together. Let's make the software answer to you.* The
shareable artifact is then a real account of change: "Our group needed
this. We made this decision. This is running now. These are the results."
Another circle can adopt what worked and adapt what legitimately needs to
differ. Only after that repetition would I generalize the tooling into a
system for starting further Product Cells.

**What "people first" must mean economically.** Capital can help build
OURS. Its role should be bounded by the purpose people choose to sustain.
Builders, operators, moderators, and support staff need to be paid. The
founder should have a sustainable livelihood. The product needs reserves
and maintenance. My proposed model would begin with founder-funded
validation, followed by transparent service funding from participants or
their organizations once useful outcomes exist. Any subsidized access
should have a visible funding source. The budget should answer a simple
question: "What does keeping this useful and dependable cost us?" The
membership structure, capital terms, and ownership transfer remain
constitutional and legal design questions. I would settle enough of that
transfer path early that ownership has a credible destination, while
avoiding promises of rights that have not been issued.

**What I would do before expanding the code.** I would run a bounded
discovery and manual service pilot: (1) choose a reachable circle; observe
a recurring workflow and collect concrete failures of its current tools;
(2) deliver the outcome manually — curate requests, facilitate
introductions, or assemble a digest using information participants
intentionally provide; (3) test participation through a real tradeoff, for
example wider opportunity visibility versus greater privacy; record the
reasoning and dissent; (4) write the narrow product mandate from that
evidence; build the repeated bottleneck through the existing kernel; (5)
demonstrate correction and continuity — reverse a problematic change and
rehearse handing operation to another capable person. I would set a budget
and evaluation criteria before beginning. The decisive evidence would be
repeated voluntary use, useful outcomes, willingness to sustain the
service, and someone besides the founder taking operational responsibility.
If people praise ownership but do not use the service, the product
hypothesis has failed. If they use it but cannot exercise meaningful
control, the institutional hypothesis remains unproven. I would defer
additional feed customization and generalized governance machinery until
those tests require them.

**About the opportunity window.** I think there is an opportunity worth
pursuing urgently. I do not think we have evidence that it is a uniquely
short window. AI-assisted development may change what a small team can
deliver, but the gains are task-dependent and difficult to measure; METR's
updated research explicitly discusses that uncertainty. (Reviewer's link:
metr.org, uplift update of 24 February 2026 — unconfirmed.) We should
record OURS' actual building and operating costs rather than make cheap
code a premise that cannot be challenged. The urgency I would act on is
this: start earning a community's trust through useful work now. Better
models will also be available to competitors. A practiced institution with
people willing to sustain it takes a different kind of effort.

My founder bet would be to make a modest but consequential piece of
professional life work better — and place real control over its
continuation with the people using it. Then the campaign can point to
something: *The software we live in should be OURS. Here is where we
started. Come build what comes next.*
